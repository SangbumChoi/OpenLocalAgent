                                                         Abstract
1              State-of-the-art tool-calling agents are built on large language models, and the
2              ﬁeld’s attention follows them, so the abilities of models in the tens of millions
3              of parameters are rarely examined. We give an end-to-end recipe for a 10 to
4              95M parameter tool caller, spanning pretraining, midtraining, ﬁne-tuning, and
5              episode-level supervision, and price each ingredient against nineteen released
6              checkpoints under one harness on ten public benchmarks. Data and additional pre-
7              training compute transfer selectively: an imported split improves predominantly
8              the benchmarks whose tool inventory it shares, and longer pretraining carries
9              never-trained suites past chance. Naïve cross-shape weight projection does not
10             help under the maps tested. Coordinate-preserving projections from larger models
11             do not improve downstream suite scores over random initialisation, though same-
12             shape weights transfer strongly. Single-call accuracy does not yield task comple-
13             tion in our simulator: all single-call-trained checkpoints complete zero multi-step
14             episodes. Episode supervision at a 12.5% mixture share lifts completion to 17.6 to
15             34.3% without lowering the ten-suite mean, and an oracle decomposition identi-
16             ﬁes argument ﬁdelity, rather than tool ordering, as the larger remaining bottleneck
17             in this simulator.


18   1 Introduction
19   Research on tool-calling agents advances on the back of large language models, and open releases
20   follow the same curve. General-purpose open-weight families commonly bottom out near 0.6B pa-
21   rameters [9,10], and the smallest general instruction-tuned baseline here is 135M [8]. Specialists
22   such as the 45M needle2 [37] reach lower and accept runtime tool schemas. What remains rare is
23   a controlled, stage-by-stage study of catalog-conditioned models from ten to a hundred million pa-
24   rameters, even though smartphones, Raspberry Pis and low-power embedded systems carry latency
25   budgets independent of the release curve.
26   Shrinking past the line is easy; one cuts width, depth, vocabulary and context until a design ﬁts
27   100M. Making the result worth deploying is hard, because choosing a custom architecture forfeits
28   compatible released weights. No released pretrained checkpoint shares the newly chosen shape, and
29   everything the model knows must be put there by training. We ask how tool calling against live
30   catalogs, and the multi-step task completion a deployed agent needs, are trained into models of 10
31   to 95M parameters, and what each ingredient of the recipe is worth.
32   We answer by measurement, with a custom family of 10.5M, 43M and 95.3M models as the in-
33   strument. One harness applies a single prompt contract, parser and set of chance ﬂoors to nineteen
34   released checkpoints on ten public agent benchmarks. Fine-tuning is matched in the controlled
35   comparisons; the stronger ﬁve-source shipped recipe is labelled separately. Decode throughput is
36   reported alongside accuracy on one server CPU at four threads.

46   2 Related work

47   Small models as agents. Tool use in LLMs descends from Toolformer [52], ReAct [53] and Gorilla
48   [54]; this report asks what survives two orders of magnitude below their usual scale. Belcak et al.
49   [14] argue that repetitive, schema-constrained agent calls suit small models, but their range is 1B to
50   7B. We study a three-size 10M to 95M family under one intervention ledger; the gap is controlled
51   evidence at this scale, not checkpoint availability itself.
52   Decomposition into small specialists. Shen et al. [15] show a single small model is a weak tool
53   learner and recover performance by splitting the agent into separately trained planner, caller and
54   summariser models; Żywot et al. [16] report that orchestrated collectives of small agents can beat
55   one larger model. Both add structure at inference time; we use one model per decision, without a
56   planner-caller-summariser ensemble.
57   Edge deployment of tool callers. TinyAgent [13] ﬁne-tunes 1.1B and 7B models and uses tool
58   retrieval to shorten prompts. MobileLLM [26] designs sub-billion architectures and includes API-
59   calling evaluation, although tool calling is not its primary training focus. Octopus v2 [27] maps a
60   ﬁxed function pool to dedicated functional tokens, reducing context and selection latency while re-
61   quiring added tokens and further training to extend the pool. Hammer [28] targets cross-benchmark
62   robustness with irrelevant-function augmentation and function masking. These systems span a con-
63   tinuum rather than a strict ﬁxed-versus-prompt binary. Our models and the current needle2 [37] both
64   accept runtime tool descriptions; needle2 additionally retrieves a top-ﬁve subset and applies schema-
65   compiled constrained decoding, whereas we study free-generation catalog conditioning across train-
66   ing stages and multi-step episodes.
67   Multi-step tool use. BUTTON [55] synthesises compositional multi-turn trajectories, PARL-MT
68   [56] trains progress awareness, and tau-bench [57] and ToolSandbox [39] evaluate stateful comple-
69   tion rather than isolated calls. We ask a narrower scale question: whether a sparse episode mixture
70   creates completion at 10 to 95M parameters, and what it costs on the single-call suites.
71   Weight reuse across shapes. Net2Net [18] and bert2BERT [19] grow a trained network function-
72   preservingly; LiGO [20] learns the growth operator; HyperCloning [21] and Weight Subcloning
73   [22] transfer initialisation between sizes. Wiring Beats Blending [58] shows within Pythia that
74   dense maps destroy structure while selection plus compensation can retain initialisation value. Our
75   transfer cell is different: a cross-family, cross-architecture coordinate-preserving slice tested as a
76   substitute for pretraining (§4, App. B). At ﬁner granularity, Yu et al. [46] identify individual super
77   weights; our band interventions ask whether update magnitude predicts which regions are useful to
78   transfer or adapt.

127   With the baseline tradeoffs established, each intervention in Table 2 runs against the control isolating
128   it, chain and hyper-parameters ﬁxed. Terms: adoption copies weights between identical shapes,

142   Covered-inventory data transfers; the tested cross-shape projections do not. Adding one open
143   split (xLAM’s own, 18,000 rows after a prompt-hash check against the evaluation shards) moves
144   xLAM 26.7 points, 3.4× its spread, from below chance-in-catalog to well above it. Relabelling that
145   corpus with the strongest ﬁne-tuned teacher (sequence-level distillation [23], the rung that remains
146   when vocabularies differ [24]), keeping the teacher’s call only where it agrees with the gold, recov-
147   ers the device-control points widening cost, inside their spreads, so suggestive only. Projecting a
148   differently-shaped open checkpoint into our model clears nothing on any open-catalog suite under
149   the coordinate-preserving maps we test; a learned growth operator in the LiGO style [20] is the
150   untested alternative.
151   The dominant measured mechanism is inventory coverage, not broad catalog skill. xLAM rises
152   26.7 while ToolACE, equally open-catalog, does not move: 99.4% of xLAM’s evaluation tool names
153   occur in the imported split, against 1.0% of ToolACE’s, and the same import moves ToolBench,
154   0.0% of whose gold tools it covers, from 12.4 to 11.5, i.e. nowhere. The import primarily teaches
155   the inventory the evaluation will ask about, rather than a general method for reading an unfamiliar
156   one, though the resulting behaviour is not mere lookup: renaming its tools costs the 96M rung
157   under four points and the 11M rung thirty-two (App. B). Hammer [28] documents this name-keyed
158   shortcutting [17]; the audit turns it into a number to compute before training, the evaluation set’s
159   tool-name coverage of any imported split. What data import never bought, longer pretraining did:
160   the same 465M-token corpus run for 4.5 epochs (16,000 steps against 1,599, 3.3 h) moves ToolACE
161   +12.1 and ToolBench +9.0 (each clearing its spread), BFCL +11.7 inside its wide one, and lifts both
162   never-trained suites above their chance-in-catalog ﬂoors for the ﬁrst time. A fresh-corpus arm at the
163   same step count reproduces the ToolACE, xLAM and BFCL gains, whereas ToolBench retains its
164   gain only with repeated text (App. C). Coverage and compute compose approximately on ToolACE
165   (+8.0, +12.1, +20.1 together), while the combined arm gives back device-control points. Because
166   BFCL and ToolBench supply no training rows, those gains cannot be direct adaptation to their train
167   splits; they are consistent with improved catalog reading, subject to the corpus audit in App. A.

180   5 From calls to task completion, and what the recipe costs

200   6 Stress tests, general cost, and claim boundaries
201   Is the xLAM gain keyed to names? Rewriting only names and gold answers separates catalog
202   reading from lookup. LocalAgent-96M is robust: 90.1 unmodiﬁed, 83.4 with opaque identiﬁers
203   and 86.3 with names rotated; LocalAgent-11M falls from 73.1 to 47.9/41.4. The shortcut is not
204   family-speciﬁc: Qwen3-0.6B moves 97.5→93.5/44.7, and SmolLM2-135M to 59.3/45.3. Coverage
205   predicts which suites move, but the 96M result survives name erasure and rotation; memorised
206   lookup is the smaller rung’s mechanism, not the imported inventory’s only use.
207   Fresh text tests whether longer pretraining is merely repetition. At the same 16,000-step bud-
208   get, a correctly packed fresh-token arm reproduces the repeated 465M-token stream’s ToolACE,
209   xLAM and BFCL gains, but gives back 8.1 of ToolBench’s 9.0 points, beyond that suite’s 6.4-point
210   spread; device-control differences stay inside spread. Extra optimisation explains three suites, while
211   ToolBench is repetition-keyed. Neither corpus is randomised, and domain overlap can survive the
212   prompt-hash audit.

371   Whether that ﬂoor does statistical work, and where it does not. It is a range over replicates, not
372   a conﬁdence interval, and Table 2 reads 21 interventions against 9 readable suites, so multiplicity
373   across 189 comparisons is a fair concern. Under a two-proportion z test on each cell’s own pool
374   sizes, the band is stricter than a Bonferroni-corrected test on most cells but not on all. Of 210
375   comparisons the band bolds 34; 28 clear Bonferroni at α = .05/189 (z > 3.48) and 6 do not: 4 on

378   pool is small, so two or three points is large against the replicate band and small against the interval

388   Which noise source binds, per suite. The band prices training-run variance, not how precisely a
389   suite can measure a rate — its pool size ﬁxes that; below, the 95% Wilson interval for a cell on each

392   What the cost column measures, and what it does not. One server CPU at four threads, fp16,

463   What the cross-shape result does and does not cover. The negative result is speciﬁc to coordinate-
464   preserving projections — leading-block truncation, head-slicing and this appendix’s size-ratio width
465   crops — which carry nothing across an architecture boundary that random initialisation does not al-
466   ready give on the downstream suites. Untested methods that could each change the answer: a learned
467   projection or growth operator in the LiGO style [20], hidden-state or feature distillation, logit distil-
468   lation, alignment of layers before mapping, low-rank factorisation of the donor, function-preserving
469   reductions, structured pruning with re-training, and tokenizer-aware embedding conversion; only the

471   ferring strongly, the supported statement is that naive cross-shape slicing fails, not that cross-shape

473   What transfers is not evenly distributed. Section 4 says the layers ﬁne-tuning writes hardest are
474   not the layers worth adapting, a statement about update magnitude within one model, not that no

476   catalog suites against random initialisation, adopting the ﬁrst half of the blocks gains 0.1 points

480   reusable, these arms do not separate and we do not claim; they establish that the transferable content

547   Three harness faults changed baseline numbers, each in our favour until ﬁxed. LFM2-350M’s
548   separate chat_template.jinja was dropped by a pattern-limited snapshot, so the harness fell

553   Batched decoding. Every model is decoded in batches of 32. For our architecture this is exactly
554   free: rows are grouped by identical token length (RoPE broadcasts one absolute-position vector, so
555   left padding would mis-position short rows), and generations are token-identical to the one-at-a-time
556   path on a 95.3M and a 10.5M checkpoint. For open models it is not free: batch size changes GEMM
557   reduction order, ﬂipping near-tied early tokens — over 710 cells, 204 up, 172 down, mean −0.005,
558   mean absolute 0.36 (SmolLM2-135M at most 1.3, Pythia-160M up to 9.9 on Mind2Web). It is a
559   protocol choice, applied to the whole open block; no table mixes the two (Granite-4.0-H’s batch-4
560   scoring exception: App. B). Re-scoring an unchanged checkpoint reproduces every suite exactly, so
561   the harness is deterministic under a ﬁxed batch size.
